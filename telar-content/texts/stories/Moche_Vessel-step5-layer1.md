The vessel's characteristic "stirrup-shaped" handle and spout make it rather difficult to fill with liquid or pour from, creating a very small aperture through which the bottle can be filled, subsequently releasing its contents in small doses. Due to their unique and admittedly impractical form, these vessels are often hypothesized to have been symbols of power or status; however, the ubiquitous nature of their production and apparent access by individuals of varying status may refute this claim. This may suggest that the vessels' primary function was aesthetic and decorative in nature. It has alternatively been proposed that the controlled administration of the vessels' contents may have been an intentional and desired feature of their design, serving to regulate the amount of a potent solution such as a hallucinogenic potion. Ethnohistorical accounts from Spanish colonists at the time of contact provide evidence that these cultures ingested plants with psychoactive properties for ceremonial purposes, and archaeological evidence from surrounding and preceding cultures depicts similar vessel forms alongside what appear to be hallucinatory substances in ceremonial contexts. However, no direct evidence from residue analysis of these stirrup-spout vessels has been found to confirm their use as vessels for administering psychoactive compounds.
