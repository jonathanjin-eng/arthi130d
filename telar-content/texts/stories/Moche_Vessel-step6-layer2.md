The claim has also been made that such vessel forms served simply as a "canvas" for figurative and pictorial depiction, however, the many examples of largely undecorated stirrup-spout bottles that have been found contest this oversimplification. On some examples of Moche pottery, including stirrup-spout vessels, fine-line drawings depict skeletons and deceased figures dancing and feasting while possessing these unique vessel forms, suggesting that the deceased were thought to require feasting implements in the afterlife. Evidence for their use in household contexts through the discovery of sherds belonging to stirrup spouted bottles as well as the observation of complete vessel forms in storage caches in such contexts indicates that these objects may have been earthly possessions before death and were subsequently included as grave goods. Furthermore, stirrup-spout vessels are archaeologically evidenced as among the foremost fineware forms within the Moche funerary tradition. Though scholars have yet to determine whether these vessels were primarily utilitarian or symbolic in their intended function, and most likely served a multitude of purposes across varying contexts, their consistent appearance as grave goods indicate that they held considerable ritual significance for the cultures that produced them.
