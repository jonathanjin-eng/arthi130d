![Moche Vessel](/telar-content/objects/Fineline_Vessel.jpg){med}
**Figure 4.** Example Moche Vessel (Right View), 100–800 CE. Ceramic, H: 26.67 cm, L: 15.24 cm. Fowler Museum at UCLA.

There seems to be a generally understood way to construct stirrup-spout vessels such as this one. The process starts with collecting clay; most red-toned pots at the time were made from terracotta. Two molds would be used. The first was a round shape used to make the body of the vessel. A layer of clay would be placed over it and smoothed out. From there, a seam would be cut into the side to slide the mold out. The potter would use slip to smooth out the seam, making it one cohesive piece. A mold would also be used to roll the spout portion of the vessel. From there, two holes would be carved into the clay of the body to attach the spout. Once smoothed out, the vessel would be decorated with slip; oftentimes, the specific makeup of this material would vary between groups of people. For this vessel, because of its simplicity, techniques like overpainting or ghost lining were most likely not used. Once painted, the vessel would be fired, a process done by placing the vessel into a pit surrounded by burning materials, most often wood and leaves. Upon retrieving the vessel from being fired, the process of the creation would be complete.
